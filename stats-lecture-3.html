Probability Foundations, Gaussian Distributions & Z-Scores

What is this topic? This module bridges probability theory with data modeling: the Addition and Multiplication rules (mutually exclusive vs. non-mutually exclusive, independent vs. dependent, and conditional probability), Permutations ($^n P_r$) vs. Combinations ($^n C_r$), the symmetrical Gaussian (Normal) Distribution bell curve, the Empirical Rule ($68-95-99.7\%$), Z-Scores ($Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$), reading Z-Tables to compute tail and body probabilities, and applying Standardization vs. Min-Max Normalization to prepare real-world features for machine learning.
Why do we use it? Natural measurements—from human heights to financial sensor errors—naturally cluster in symmetrical bell curves. By transforming raw measurements into standardized Z-scores, we can compare metrics measured on completely different scales (like comparing cricket batting averages across different pitch conditions, or combining age in years with salary in dollars without allowing large salary numbers to overpower machine learning algorithms).

01. Probability Foundations: Sample Spaces & Likelihood

1. What is Probability?

Probability is the numerical measure of the likelihood that an event will occur. Its value is strictly bounded between $0$ (impossible event) and $1$ (certain event):

$$P(\text{Event}) = \frac{\text{Number of Favorable Outcomes}}{\text{Total Number of Possible Outcomes in Sample Space } S}$$

2. Basic Experiments & Sample Spaces

  • Rolling a Fair 6-Sided Die: Sample Space $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$. Probability of rolling a $6$: $$P(\text{Rolling a } 6) = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.1667 \ (16.67\%)$$
  • Flipping an Unbiased Coin: Sample Space $S = \{\text{Head}, \text{Tail}\}$. $$P(\text{Head}) = \frac{1}{2} = 0.50 \ (50\%)$$

02. The Addition Rule (Mutually Exclusive vs. Non-Mutually Exclusive)

1. Mutually Exclusive Events

Two events are Mutually Exclusive (disjoint) if they cannot occur at the same time. (e.g. In a single coin flip, you cannot get both Heads and Tails simultaneously).

$$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

example 
Question: What is the probability of rolling a 1, 3, or 6 on a single die roll?
P(1 or 3 or 6) = P(1) + P(3) + P(6) = 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 = 3/6 = 0.50 (50%)

2. Non-Mutually Exclusive Events

Events are Non-Mutually Exclusive if both can happen at the same time (there is an intersection or overlap $A \cap B$). If we simply added their probabilities, the overlapping elements would be counted twice!

$$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

Card Deck Example from Lecture: Queen or Heart

In a standard 52-card deck, what is the probability of randomly drawing a card that is either a Queen or a Heart?

  • Total cards $n = 52$
  • Number of Queens: $4 \implies P(\text{Queen}) = \frac{4}{52}$
  • Number of Hearts: $13 \implies P(\text{Heart}) = \frac{13}{52}$
  • Number of cards that are BOTH a Queen and a Heart: $1$ (Queen of Hearts) $\implies P(\text{Queen} \cap \text{Heart}) = \frac{1}{52}$

$$P(\text{Queen or Heart}) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{13}{52} - \frac{1}{52} = \frac{16}{52} = \frac{4}{13} \approx \mathbf{30.77\%}$$

03. The Multiplication Rule & Conditional Probability

1. Independent Events

Two events are Independent if the occurrence of event $A$ has no influence on the probability of event $B$.

$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A \cap B) = P(A) \times P(B)$$

Example: What is the probability of rolling a $5$ on the first die and a $4$ on the second die?
$$P(5 \text{ and } 4) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{36} \approx \mathbf{2.78\%}$$

2. Dependent Events & Conditional Probability

Events are Dependent when event $A$ directly changes the probability space for event $B$. The probability of $B$ given that $A$ has already occurred is written $P(B \mid A)$:

$$P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B \mid A)$$

Marble Bag Example from Lecture (Without Replacement)

A bag contains 3 Red Marbles and 2 Green Marbles (Total = 5). What is the probability of drawing a Green marble first, and then a Red marble without putting the first marble back?

  • First draw: $P(\text{Green}) = \frac{2}{5}$
  • Second draw: 4 marbles remain in the bag (3 Red, 1 Green).
    Conditional probability: $P(\text{Red} \mid \text{Green}) = \frac{3}{4}$
  • Joint Probability: $$P(\text{Green and Red}) = \frac{2}{5} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{6}{20} = \mathbf{0.30 \ (30\%)}$$

This concept of updating probabilities based on prior events is the theoretical foundation of Bayes' Theorem and the Naive Bayes Machine Learning Classifier!

04. Permutations ($^n P_r$) vs. Combinations ($^n C_r$)

Technique Does Order Matter? Mathematical Formula Real-Life Example
Permutation ($^n P_r$) YES (Order matters). $AB \neq BA$. $$^n P_r = \frac{n!}{(n - r)!}$$ Lock passcodes, podium finishes (1st, 2nd, 3rd), diary logging order.
Combination ($^n C_r$) NO (Order does NOT matter). $AB = BA$. $$^n C_r = \frac{n!}{r! (n - r)!}$$ Selecting team members, choosing 3 books to take on vacation.

The Chocolate Factory Problem from Lecture

A candy factory produces $n = 6$ distinct brands: [Dairy Milk, 5-Star, Milkybar, Eclairs, Gems, Silk]. A student is instructed to record the first $r = 3$ chocolates seen:

  • If Order Matters (Permutation): $$^6 P_3 = \frac{6!}{(6 - 3)!} = \frac{6 \times 5 \times 4 \times 3!}{3!} = 6 \times 5 \times 4 = \mathbf{120 \text{ arrangements}}$$
  • If Taking 3 Unique Chocolates Home (Combination): $$^6 C_3 = \frac{6!}{3! (6 - 3)!} = \frac{6 \times 5 \times 4}{3 \times 2 \times 1} = \mathbf{20 \text{ unique selections}}$$

05. The Gaussian (Normal) Distribution & Bell Curve Anatomy

1. What is the Normal Distribution?

The Normal Distribution (or Gaussian Distribution) is a continuous probability distribution characterized by a symmetrical, bell-shaped curve. It is described completely by two parameters: its Mean ($\mu$) and Standard Deviation ($\sigma$):

$$X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$$

2. Fundamental Properties of the Bell Curve

  • Perfect Symmetry: The curve is completely symmetrical about the center line $\mu$. Exactly $50\%$ of all data points lie to the left of the mean, and $50\%$ lie to the right.
  • Coincidence of Centers: At the central apex peak, $\text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \text{Mode}$.
  • Asymptotic Tails: The tails approach the horizontal axis infinitely closely in both directions ($-\infty$ and $+\infty$), but never touch it.
  • Total Area Invariant: The total area under the curve is strictly equal to $1.0$ ($100\%$).

06. The Empirical Rule ($68 - 95 - 99.7\%$ Rule)

For any dataset that follows a Gaussian normal distribution, the distribution of observations relative to standard deviation follows universal mathematical constants:

68.27%
Within 1 Standard Deviation

$$\mu \pm 1\sigma = [\mu - \sigma, \ \mu + \sigma]$$ Roughly 68 out of every 100 observations fall within this central zone.

95.45%
Within 2 Standard Deviations

$$\mu \pm 2\sigma = [\mu - 2\sigma, \ \mu + 2\sigma]$$ Over 95 out of every 100 observations fall within 2 standard deviations.

99.73%
Within 3 Standard Deviations

$$\mu \pm 3\sigma = [\mu - 3\sigma, \ \mu + 3\sigma]$$ Practically all data (99.73%) falls inside. Anything beyond $|3\sigma|$ is an outlier!

07. The Z-Score: Standardizing Distance from the Mean

1. What is a Z-Score?

A Z-Score (or standard score) tells you exactly how many standard deviations an observation $X$ is located above or below the mean:

$$Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$$

2. Interpreting the Sign of a Z-Score

  • $Z = 0$: The point is exactly at the mean ($X = \mu$).
  • $Z > 0$ (Positive): The point lies to the right (above the mean).
  • $Z < 0$ (Negative): The point lies to the left (below the mean).
python 
# Lecture Example: mu = 4, sigma = 1
mu = 4
sigma = 1

# Case 1: X = 4.75
z1 = (4.75 - mu) / sigma  # z = +0.75 (0.75 sigma to the right)

# Case 2: X = 3.75
z2 = (3.75 - mu) / sigma  # z = -0.25 (0.25 sigma to the left)

08. The Standard Normal Distribution ($\mu = 0, \sigma = 1$)

1. The Universal Transformation

Every dataset in the universe has its own raw units and mean: temperatures in Fahrenheit, salaries in rupees, heights in centimeters. When you subtract the mean and divide by standard deviation for every data point, you map that distribution into the Standard Normal Distribution:

$$Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \quad \text{where } \mu_z = 0 \text{ and } \sigma_z = 1$$

09. Master Guide: How to Read Z-Tables

1. Cumulative Left-Tail vs. Right-Tail Area

Standard statistical Z-tables provide the cumulative area to the left of a given $Z$ value ($P(Z \le z)$). Because the total area under the curve is strictly $1.0$:

$$\text{Right-Tail Area } P(Z > z) = 1 - P(Z \le z)$$

2. Solved Problem 1: Percentage of Scores Above 4.25

Given $\mu = 4, \sigma = 1$. What percentage of scores fall above $4.25$?

  1. Compute Z-score: $Z = \frac{4.25 - 4}{1} = \mathbf{0.25}$.
  2. Look up $Z = 0.25$ in standard Z-table:
    • Row 0.2, Column 0.05 $\implies \text{Left Area} = \mathbf{0.5987}$ ($59.87\%$).
  3. We want the area above (right tail): $$\text{Area} = 1 - 0.5987 = \mathbf{0.4013} \approx \mathbf{40.13\%}$$

3. Solved Problem 2: Indian Population IQ Problem

In a population study, average IQ is $\mu = 100$ with $\sigma = 15$. What percentage of people have an IQ lower than 85?

  1. Compute Z-score: $Z = \frac{85 - 100}{15} = \frac{-15}{15} = \mathbf{-1.00}$.
  2. Look up $Z = -1.00$ in negative Z-table:
    • Row -1.0, Column 0.00 $\implies \text{Area} = \mathbf{0.1587}$.
  3. Conclusion: Exactly $\mathbf{15.87\%}$ of the population is expected to have an IQ below 85.

10. Feature Preprocessing in ML: Standardization vs. Min-Max Normalization

1. Why Feature Scaling is Mandatory in Machine Learning

Consider training a machine learning model on customer records with three features:

  • Age: Ranges from 18 to 70 (Scale of tens).
  • Annual Salary: Ranges from \$30,000 to \$250,000 (Scale of hundreds of thousands).
  • Weight: Ranges from 50 kg to 100 kg.

Distance-based algorithms (KNN, SVM, K-Means, PCA, and Gradient Descent) calculate Euclidean distances: $d = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_2)^2 + (y_1 - y_2)^2}$. Because salary numbers are thousands of times larger than age numbers, the algorithm will completely ignore age and weight! We must rescale them.

Criteria Standardization (StandardScaler) Normalization (Min-Max Scaler)
Formula $$X_{\text{std}} = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$$ $$X_{\text{norm}} = \frac{X - X_{\min}}{X_{\max} - X_{\min}}$$
Output Range Unbounded (typically $-3$ to $+3$). Centers at $\mu = 0, \sigma = 1$. Strictly bounded between $[0, 1]$ (or $[-1, 1]$).
Outlier Sensitivity Less sensitive. Outliers are retained in the outer standard deviations. Highly sensitive! An extreme outlier will crush all other normal data points into a tiny cluster near 0.
Primary ML Use Cases Linear Regression, Logistic Regression, SVM, KNN, PCA. Deep Learning Neural Networks (CNNs, image pixels $0-255 \to [0, 1]$).

11. Python Implementation: Detecting Outliers via $|Z| > 3$

python 
import numpy as np

def detect_outliers_zscore(data, threshold=3):
    \"\"\"Identify data points falling beyond threshold standard deviations from mean.\"\"\"
    mean = np.mean(data)
    std = np.std(data)
    
    outliers = []
    for x in data:
        z_score = (x - mean) / std
        if np.abs(z_score) > threshold:
            outliers.append(x)
    return outliers

# Test dataset from lecture
dataset = [11, 10, 12, 14, 12, 15, 14, 13, 15, 102, 12, 14, 17, 19, 107, 10, 13, 12, 14, 12, 108, 12, 11, 14, 13, 15, 10, 15, 12, 10, 14, 13, 15, 10]
found_outliers = detect_outliers_zscore(dataset, threshold=3)
print("Outliers detected by Z-score:", found_outliers)
# Output: Outliers detected by Z-score: [102, 107, 108]
