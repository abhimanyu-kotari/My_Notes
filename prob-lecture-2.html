Joint, Marginal, Conditional Probability & Bayes' Theorem

What is this topic? This lecture covers multi-variable probability theory: how two or more random variables interact simultaneously. We explore the two primary visual tools for multi-event analysis (Venn diagrams and Contingency Tables), define the three foundational types of probability (Joint $P(X \cap Y)$, Marginal $P(X)$, and Conditional $P(A \mid B)$), unpack the concept of sample space reduction, clarify the vital differences between Independent, Dependent, and Mutually Exclusive events, derive the algebraic proof of Bayes' Theorem, and implement a complete Naive Bayes classification decision rule from scratch on tabular data.
Why do we use it? Real-world machine learning models never look at a single feature in isolation; they analyze hundreds of features simultaneously. When an ML model predicts medical diagnoses, filters spam, or computes fraud risk, it relies on Bayes' Theorem to rationally update prior probabilities using incoming evidence. Without understanding joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities, you cannot comprehend classification algorithms, loss functions, or Bayesian machine learning.

01. Roadmap & The Architecture of Multi-Variable Probability

In Lecture 1, we mastered the core foundations: single random experiments, sample spaces $S$, random variables as functions $X: S \to \mathbb{R}$, probability distribution tables (PMFs), expected value ($\mathbb{E}[X]$), and variance ($\text{Var}(X)$).

However, real data science problems rarely involve a single random variable. In datasets like Titanic, you have passenger class (`Pclass`), passenger sex (`Sex`), ticket fare (`Fare`), and survival status (`Survived`). To model how features relate to targets, we need the mathematics of multi-variable probability.

02. Visual Foundation 1: Venn Diagrams & Set Operations

Before analyzing numbers in tables, probability theory uses Venn Diagrams from Set Theory to visualize how events intersect, combine, and partition the sample space.

Mapping Set Theory to Probability Theory

  • The Universal Set ($U$): Represented by the outer rectangular bounding box. In probability, this is the Sample Space ($S$) containing all possible outcomes. Its total probability is always $P(U) = P(S) = 1.0$.
  • Subsets / Circles ($A, B$): Represent individual Events consisting of favorable outcomes.
  • Complement ($A'$ or $A^c$): Everything outside circle $A$ but inside the bounding box: $P(A') = 1 - P(A)$.
  • Intersection ($A \cap B$): The shared overlapping lens where both events occur simultaneously.
  • Union ($A \cup B$): The total combined area covered by $A$, $B$, or both.

The Addition Rule for Any Two Events

When you add the area of circle $A$ to the area of circle $B$, the overlapping intersection $A \cap B$ is counted twice! To prevent double-counting, you must subtract the intersection once:

$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$$

Concrete Walkthrough: A Single 6-Sided Die Roll

Let the experiment be rolling a fair 6-sided die: $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ ($n(S) = 6$).

  • Event $A$: Rolling a number greater than or equal to 4 $\implies A = \{4, 5, 6\} \implies P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.50$.
  • Event $B$: Rolling an even number $\implies B = \{2, 4, 6\} \implies P(B) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.50$.
  • Intersection ($A \cap B$): Numbers that are BOTH $\ge 4$ AND even $\implies A \cap B = \{4, 6\} \implies P(A \cap B) = \frac{2}{6} = \frac{1}{3}$.
  • Union ($A \cup B$): Numbers that are either $\ge 4$ OR even $\implies A \cup B = \{2, 4, 5, 6\} \implies n(A \cup B) = 4$.
    Using formula: $P(A \cup B) = \frac{3}{6} + \frac{3}{6} - \frac{2}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}} \approx 0.667$.
  • Complement of Union ($(A \cup B)'$): Numbers outside both circles $\implies \{1, 3\} \implies P = 1 - \frac{2}{3} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{3}}$.

03. Visual Foundation 2: Contingency Tables (Cross-Tabs)

While Venn diagrams provide high-level geometric intuition, they become messy when dealing with large numbers, percentages, or multi-class features. In data analysis and machine learning, we use Contingency Tables (also known as Cross-Tabulations or two-way tables).

💡
Lossless Equivalence

Every piece of information represented in a two-circle Venn diagram can be transferred into a $2 \times 2$ Contingency Table with zero loss of information, and vice-versa!

Walkthrough: The 100-Student Electives Class

Consider a university cohort of $100$ students choosing between two elective subjects: Mathematics and Biology.

  • $10$ students enrolled in both Math and Biology.
  • $30$ students enrolled in only Math (not Biology).
  • $40$ students enrolled in only Biology (not Math).
  • Remaining students took neither subject: $100 - (10 + 30 + 40) = \mathbf{20}$ students.

Here is how this exact problem maps into a clean Contingency Table:

Elective Choice Biology ($\text{Bio}$) Not Biology ($\text{Bio}'$) Total (Math Margins)
Mathematics ($\text{Math}$) 10 (Both) 30 (Math only) 40
Not Mathematics ($\text{Math}'$) 40 (Bio only) 20 (Neither) 60
Total (Bio Margins) 50 50 100 (Grand Total)

Notice the mathematical elegance: $$\text{Math Margin: } 40 + 60 = 100 \qquad \text{Bio Margin: } 50 + 50 = 100$$ Contingency tables provide the exact substrate needed to calculate Joint, Marginal, and Conditional probabilities in seconds.

04. Joint Probability: Simultaneous Multi-Variable Events

Definition of Joint Probability

Joint Probability is the probability of two or more events occurring simultaneously (at the exact same time) in a single random trial. For two random variables $X$ and $Y$:

$$P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x \text{ and } Y = y) = P(X \cap Y)$$

In machine learning, this answers queries like: "What is the probability that a customer belongs to Age Group 20-30 AND clicked on the banner ad?"

Titanic Dataset Walkthrough ($N = 891$)

Let $X$ be passenger class (`Pclass` $\in \{1, 2, 3\}$) and $Y$ be survival status (`Survived` $\in \{0 = \text{Died}, 1 = \text{Survived}\}$).

The raw observed passenger counts in the dataset form our contingency table:

Passenger Class ($X$) Died ($Y = 0$) Survived ($Y = 1$) Total Passengers
1st Class ($X = 1$) 80 136 216
2nd Class ($X = 2$) 97 87 184
3rd Class ($X = 3$) 372 119 491
Total 549 342 891

To convert these raw counts into Joint Probabilities, we divide each individual cell by the grand total $N = 891$:

Passenger Class ($X$) $P(X = x, Y = 0)$ [Died] $P(X = x, Y = 1)$ [Survived]
1st Class ($X = 1$) $\frac{80}{891} = \mathbf{0.0898}$ $\frac{136}{891} = \mathbf{0.1526}$
2nd Class ($X = 2$) $\frac{97}{891} = \mathbf{0.1089}$ $\frac{87}{891} = \mathbf{0.0976}$
3rd Class ($X = 3$) $\frac{372}{891} = \mathbf{0.4175}$ $\frac{119}{891} = \mathbf{0.1336}$
🎯
The Joint Distribution Axiom

The entire table above represents the Joint Probability Distribution. Summing all 6 joint cells equals exactly 1.0:

$$0.0898 + 0.1526 + 0.1089 + 0.0976 + 0.4175 + 0.1336 = \mathbf{1.0000}$$

In Python (Pandas), you generate this table in a single line:

python 
import pandas as pd

# Joint Probability Distribution across all cells
joint_dist = pd.crosstab(df['Pclass'], df['Survived'], normalize='all')
print(joint_dist)

05. Marginal Probability: Summing Across Margins

Also referred to in literature as Simple Probability or Unconditional Probability.

Definition of Marginal Probability

Marginal Probability refers to the probability of an event occurring completely irrespective / regardless of the outcome of any other variable. It ignores all other conditions.

$$P(X = x) = \sum_{y} P(X = x, Y = y)$$
$$P(Y = y) = \sum_{x} P(X = x, Y = y)$$

Why Is It Named "Marginal"?

The name comes from the physical layout of cross-tabulation tables! When you sum the joint probabilities across a row or down a column, the total is written in the margins (borders) of the table:

Pclass ($X$) Died ($Y = 0$) Survived ($Y = 1$) Marginal $P(X)$
1st Class ($X=1$) 0.0898 0.1526 0.2424 (24.2%)
2nd Class ($X=2$) 0.1089 0.0976 0.2065 (20.7%)
3rd Class ($X=3$) 0.4175 0.1336 0.5511 (55.1%)
Marginal $P(Y)$ 0.6162 (61.6% Died) 0.3838 (38.4% Survived) 1.0000

In Python Pandas, adding `margins=True` outputs both joint and marginal distributions together:

python 
# Combined Joint and Marginal Distributions in Pandas
prob_matrix = pd.crosstab(df['Pclass'], df['Survived'], margins=True, normalize='all')
print(prob_matrix)

06. Conditional Probability: The Philosophy of Reduced Sample Spaces

Conditional Probability is indisputably the most critical probabilistic concept in machine learning.

What is Conditional Probability?

Conditional Probability is the measure of the probability of an event $A$ occurring, given that another event $B$ has already occurred.

$$P(A \mid B)$$

Read aloud as: "The probability of A given B" or "The probability of A conditional on B".

The Core Geometric Intuition: The Shrinking Universe

How does conditional probability actually work under the hood? The secret lies in one single concept:

CONDITIONING ON EVENT $B$ SHRINKS THE SAMPLE SPACE DOWN TO $B$!

Walk through the mental progression:

  1. Before you are given any information, your universe is the entire sample space $S$. All outcomes are on the table.
  2. Suddenly, a reliable witness informs you: "Event $B$ has definitely occurred!"
  3. Instantly, every single outcome in $S$ that lies outside circle $B$ is physically impossible. They are discarded ($P = 0$).
  4. Your new universe (new denominator) is now strictly $B$.
  5. Within this restricted universe $B$, you now look for where event $A$ also occurs. But in $B$, the only place $A$ can occur is the shared lens: $A \cap B$!
  6. Therefore, the conditional probability is simply the fraction of the new universe occupied by $A$: $$\text{Proportion} = \frac{\text{Shared Region}}{\text{New Universe}} = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$
⚡
Key Insight

There is no complex higher-math derivation required. The formula $P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$ emerges directly and logically from the geometry of sample space reduction!

07. 3 Step-by-Step Solved Problems (Coins, Dice & Titanic)

Let's practice the reduced sample space technique on three classic problems discussed in the lecture:

Problem 1: Three Coins Toss (At Least Two Heads)

Question: Three fair coins are tossed. What is the conditional probability that at least 2 coins show Heads ($A$), given that at least 1 coin shows Heads ($B$)?

1. Full Sample Space (|S| = 2³ = 8):
   S = {HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT}

2. Event B occurred ("At least 1 Head"):
   TTT is eliminated! Reduced sample space has 7 outcomes:
   S_reduced = {HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH} &implies; n(B) = 7

3. Count favorable outcomes for Event A ("≥ 2 Heads") inside S_reduced:
   Favorable = {HHH, HHT, HTH, THH} &implies; n(A ∩ B) = 4

4. Compute Conditional Probability:
   P(A | B) = 4 / 7 ≈ 0.5714 (57.1%)

Problem 2: Two Dice (Sum is 7 Given First Die is Odd)

Question: Two standard dice are rolled ($|S| = 36$). Find the conditional probability that the sum of the numbers is 7 ($A$), given that the first die shows an odd number ($B$).

1. Condition B: First die is odd &implies; d1 ∈ {1, 3, 5}:
   Rows 2, 4, and 6 are completely eliminated!
   Only 3 rows remain (each with 6 pairs) &implies; n(B) = 3 × 6 = 18 outcomes.

2. Identify pairs where sum equals 7 in this reduced set of 18:
   In row 1 (d1 = 1): (1, 6) → sum = 7
   In row 3 (d1 = 3): (3, 4) → sum = 7
   In row 5 (d1 = 5): (5, 2) → sum = 7
   Total favorable pairs = 3

3. Compute Conditional Probability:
   P(A | B) = 3 / 18 = 1 / 6 ≈ 0.1667 (16.7%)

Problem 3: Two Dice (First Die is 2 Given Sum $\le 5$)

Question: Find the conditional probability that the first die rolled is 2 ($A$), given that the sum of the two dice is less than or equal to 5 ($B$).

1. Find all outcomes in S where sum ≤ 5 (Condition B):
   Sum = 2: (1,1) [1]
   Sum = 3: (1,2), (2,1) [2]
   Sum = 4: (1,3), (2,2), (3,1) [3]
   Sum = 5: (1,4), (2,3), (3,2), (4,1) [4]
   Reduced universe size n(B) = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10 outcomes.

2. In these 10 pairs, count where d1 = 2 (Event A):
   (2, 1), (2, 2), (2, 3) &implies; Exactly 3 pairs.

3. Compute Conditional Probability:
   P(A | B) = 3 / 10 = 0.3000 (30.0%)

08. The Formal Conditional Formula & Pandas Code

When dealing with massive enterprise datasets, manual sample space reduction is impossible. We use the formal algebraic formula:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{\text{Joint Probability of } A \text{ and } B}{\text{Marginal Probability of } B}$$

Titanic Real-World Analysis

Let's query the Titanic dataset using this formula:

Query 1: P(Died | 3rd Class)

"If a passenger held a 3rd class ticket, what was their probability of dying?"

P(Died ∩ Pclass=3) = 372 / 891 = 0.4175
P(Pclass=3) = 491 / 891 = 0.5511
P(Died | Pclass=3) = 0.4175 / 0.5511 = 75.76%
Query 2: P(Died | 1st Class)

"If a passenger held a 1st class ticket, what was their probability of dying?"

P(Died ∩ Pclass=1) = 80 / 891 = 0.0898
P(Pclass=1) = 216 / 891 = 0.2424
P(Died | Pclass=1) = 0.0898 / 0.2424 = 37.04%

Computing Conditional Probabilities in Pandas: Index vs. Columns Normalization

In Pandas, changing the `normalize` parameter controls the direction of conditioning:

python 
import pandas as pd

# 1. Condition on Pclass (Rows sum to 1.0): P(Survived | Pclass)
p_surv_given_class = pd.crosstab(df['Pclass'], df['Survived'], normalize='index')
print("P(Survived | Pclass):")
print(p_surv_given_class)

# 2. Reverse conditioning on Survived (Cols sum to 1.0): P(Pclass | Survived)
p_class_given_surv = pd.crosstab(df['Pclass'], df['Survived'], normalize='columns')
print("\nP(Pclass | Survived):")
print(p_class_given_surv)

Notice that $P(\text{Pclass}=3 \mid \text{Died}=0) = \frac{372}{549} \approx \mathbf{67.76\%}$. This tells us that among all the people who died, nearly 68% of them came from 3rd class!

09. Independent vs. Dependent vs. Mutually Exclusive Events

One of the most persistent misunderstandings among data scientists is conflating Independent Events with Mutually Exclusive Events. They are fundamentally different concepts!

Dimension Independent Events Dependent Events Mutually Exclusive Events
Core Definition Occurrence of $A$ has zero effect on the probability of $B$. Occurrence of $A$ changes the probability of event $B$. Events $A$ and $B$ cannot happen at the same time in a single trial.
Intersection $P(A \cap B)$ $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$ $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)$ $P(A \cap B) = 0$ ($A \cap B = \emptyset$)
Conditional $P(A \mid B)$ $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$ $P(A \mid B) \ne P(A)$ $P(A \mid B) = 0$
Classic Example Tossing a coin (Heads) and rolling a die (6); card draw with replacement. Drawing two Aces from a 52-card deck without replacement. Rolling a die and getting both a 2 and a 5 on the same roll.
⚠️
THE CRITICAL PARADOX

Can two mutually exclusive events (with non-zero probability) ever be independent?
ABSOLUTELY NOT!
If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, knowing that $A$ happened gives you 100% certainty that $B$ did not happen ($P(B \mid A) = 0$). That is maximum statistical dependence, not independence!

10. Bayes' Theorem: Mathematical Proof & Terminology

Formulated by English statistician Reverend Thomas Bayes in the 1700s, Bayes' Theorem is one of the most powerful and celebrated equations in mathematics.

The Conceptual Essence of Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' Theorem describes how to rationally update our belief in a hypothesis ($A$) when presented with new evidence or observations ($B$):

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)}$$

The 4 Formal Statistical Terms

1. Posterior Probability: $P(A \mid B)$

The updated probability of hypothesis $A$ after seeing the evidence $B$.

2. Likelihood: $P(B \mid A)$

The probability of observing evidence $B$ assuming hypothesis $A$ were true.

3. Prior Probability: $P(A)$

Our initial, baseline belief in hypothesis $A$ before seeing any new evidence.

4. Evidence (Marginal): $P(B)$

The total probability of observing evidence $B$ across all possible hypotheses.

The Complete Algebraic Derivation

Despite its profound status in artificial intelligence, the mathematical derivation of Bayes' Theorem takes only three elementary lines of algebra:

Step 1: Write the definition of conditional probability for $P(A \mid B)$:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \implies P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$

Step 2: Write the definition of conditional probability for $P(B \mid A)$:

$$P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \implies P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A) \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}$$

Step 3: Since the left-hand sides of Equation 1 and Equation 2 both equal $P(A \cap B)$, equate their right-hand sides:

$$P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)$$

Step 4: Divide both sides by $P(B)$:

$$\mathbf{P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)}} \quad \blacksquare$$

11. Machine Learning Case Study: Building a Bayes Classifier

How is Bayes' Theorem directly executed by classification algorithms like Naive Bayes? Let's walk through the end-to-end classification problem from Nitish's lecture.

The Training Dataset (5 Historical Passenger Records)

Passenger ID Gender (`Sex`) Survival (`Survived`)
1 Male 0 (Died)
2 Male 0 (Died)
3 Female 1 (Survived)
4 Female 0 (Died)
5 Male 1 (Survived)

The Query: Predict Output for a New Passenger

A new passenger boards the ship with feature: $\mathbf{X} = \text{Male}$.
Will this passenger survive ($Y = 1$) or die ($Y = 0$)?

We use Bayes' Theorem to compute the posterior probabilities for both candidate classes:

Evaluate Hypothesis 1: Died ($Y = 0$)

• Prior $P(Y = 0)$: 3 out of 5 passengers died $\implies \mathbf{3/5} = 0.60$
• Likelihood $P(\text{Male} \mid Y = 0)$: Out of the 3 dead passengers, 2 were Male $\implies \mathbf{2/3} \approx 0.667$
• Evidence $P(\text{Male})$: 3 out of 5 total passengers are Male $\implies \mathbf{3/5} = 0.60$
P(0 | Male) = [(2/3) × (3/5)] / (3/5)
P(0 | Male) = 2/3 ≈ 0.6667 (66.7%)

Evaluate Hypothesis 2: Survived ($Y = 1$)

• Prior $P(Y = 1)$: 2 out of 5 passengers survived $\implies \mathbf{2/5} = 0.40$
• Likelihood $P(\text{Male} \mid Y = 1)$: Out of the 2 survivors, 1 was Male $\implies \mathbf{1/2} = 0.50$
• Evidence $P(\text{Male})$: 3 out of 5 total passengers are Male $\implies \mathbf{3/5} = 0.60$
P(1 | Male) = [(1/2) × (2/5)] / (3/5)
P(1 | Male) = (1/5) / (3/5) = 1/3 ≈ 0.3333 (33.3%)

Final Machine Learning Decision

Since $P(\text{Died} \mid \text{Male}) = 66.7\% > P(\text{Survived} \mid \text{Male}) = 33.3\%$, the Bayes classifier predicts:

PREDICTION: DIED (0)
💡
How Naive Bayes Scales

In real datasets with multiple feature columns ($x_1 = \text{Pclass}, x_2 = \text{Sex}, x_3 = \text{Age}$), calculating the joint likelihood $P(x_1, x_2, x_3 \mid y)$ directly from data is impossible due to the curse of dimensionality. The "Naive" assumption assumes features are conditionally independent given the class:

$$P(x_1, x_2, x_3 \mid y) \approx P(x_1 \mid y) \cdot P(x_2 \mid y) \cdot P(x_3 \mid y)$$

This turns an intractable joint probability into simple products of individual 1D feature likelihoods!
