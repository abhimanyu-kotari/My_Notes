Descriptive Statistics, Data Types & Sampling Techniques

What is this topic? This module introduces the foundations of statistics for data science: understanding the difference between Descriptive and Inferential statistics, classifying data into Quantitative vs. Qualitative and Discrete vs. Continuous, measuring variables across 4 fundamental scales (Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, Ratio), selecting representative samples from populations using 4 proven techniques, and visualizing data distributions with frequency tables, bar charts, histograms, and smooth Probability Density Functions (PDF).
Why do we use it? In data science and machine learning, you cannot train reliable models or extract valid business insights from raw data without first organizing, cleaning, and summarizing it. Proper sampling prevents election-poll and medical trial bias, correct measurement scales ensure you don't perform invalid math on categories, and histograms reveal the underlying distribution shape of your features.

01. Descriptive vs. Inferential Statistics (The Data Science Divide)

1. What is Statistics?

At its core, Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, analyzing, and interpreting data. With the explosive volume of data generated by modern software, databases, and sensors, statistics provides the mathematical toolkit needed to make better, evidence-backed business decisions.

The Two Pillars of Statistics in Data Science

1. Descriptive Statistics

Techniques used to organize, summarize, and describe the characteristics of a specific dataset that has already been observed or measured.

  • Measures of Central Tendency (Mean, Median, Mode)
  • Measures of Dispersion (Variance, Standard Deviation, Range)
  • Visualizations (Histograms, Box Plots, Bar Charts, KDE)
  • Example Question: "What was the average math exam score of the 20 students in Class A?"
2. Inferential Statistics

Techniques that use sample data to make predictions, test hypotheses, and draw formal conclusions about an entire population.

  • Hypothesis Testing ($Z$-test, $t$-test, ANOVA, $\chi^2$-test)
  • Confidence Intervals & Margin of Error
  • P-values, Significance Levels ($lpha$), and Decision Rules
  • Example Question: "Are the exam scores of Class A representative of all 1,000 math students across the entire university?"
Comparison Dimension Descriptive Statistics Inferential Statistics
Primary Objective Describe and summarize the known dataset. Infer properties and test claims about an unmeasured population.
Scope of Data Applies strictly to the observed dataset in hand. Generalizes beyond the sample to a larger population.
Core Tools Mean, Median, Mode, Variance, IQR, Plots. $Z$-test, Student's $t$-test, ANOVA, Chi-Square, Confidence Intervals.
Certainty Exact calculations with 100% certainty for that data. Probabilistic conclusions involving confidence levels (e.g. 95% CI).

02. What is Data? Quantitative vs. Qualitative Variables

1. Defining Data and Variables

Data consists of raw facts, figures, or pieces of information that can be recorded and measured. A Variable is any characteristic, number, or quantity that can be measured or counted and can take on different values across individuals or entities.

2. The Two Primary Classes of Variables

Variables are divided into two fundamental branches:

Quantitative (Numerical) Variables

Variables measured numerically. You can perform standard mathematical operations on them (addition, subtraction, multiplication, averages).

Examples: Age (25, 30), Weight (72 kg), Height (178 cm), Bank Balance ($5,000), Exam Marks (85%).
Qualitative (Categorical) Variables

Variables based on descriptive characteristics, attributes, or labels. They represent groups or classes; arithmetic operations do not apply.

Examples: Gender (Male/Female), Blood Group (A+, O-), T-Shirt Size (S, M, L, XL), Flower Species (Rose, Lily).
Data Science Interview Trap: Numeric Representation of Categorical Data

Just because a feature contains numbers does not automatically make it quantitative! For example:

  • PIN Code / Zip Code: 560001 vs 110001. You cannot add two pin codes together or calculate the "average pin code" of a state. It is a categorical label encoded as digits.
  • Phone Numbers & Employee IDs: Numeric symbols acting as identifiers, not countable quantities.

03. Discrete vs. Continuous Variables

Quantitative variables are further split into two distinct subclasses:

04. The 4 Variable Measurement Scales (NOIR Framework)

In 1946, psychologist S. S. Stevens introduced the NOIR hierarchy of measurement levels. In data analysis, knowing the scale determines which statistical operations, tests, and ML encodings are legally valid.

1. Nominal Qualitative

Data categorized by labels or names. Order does not matter, and no mathematical calculations can be performed.

Examples: Gender, Eye Color, Marital Status, Flower Type.
Allowed Math: Mode, Frequency counts, Equality ($=$ or $ eq$).
2. Ordinal Qualitative

Data where order or rank matters, but the mathematical difference between consecutive ranks is unknown or unequal.

Examples: Exam Ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd), Customer Feedback (Good, Fair, Poor), T-Shirt Sizes (S, M, L).
Allowed Math: Median, Percentiles, Comparisons ($>, <$).
3. Interval Quantitative

Numeric data where order and exact differences matter, but there is NO True / Natural Zero (zero does not mean absence).

Examples: Temperature in °F or °C (0°F does not mean "no temperature"), IQ scores, Calendar years.
Allowed Math: Addition, Subtraction, Mean, Std Dev. (No ratios!).
4. Ratio Quantitative

Numeric data with order, exact differences, and a True Natural Zero point (zero means total absence of the property).

Examples: Weight (0 kg = no weight), Height, Salary, Distance, Cab fares.
Allowed Math: All arithmetic (+, -, *, /), Ratios ("100 kg is 2x 50 kg").
Why the "True Zero" Difference Matters

Consider temperature: $80^\circ ext{F}$ is not "twice as hot" as $40^\circ ext{F}$ because $0^\circ ext{F}$ is an arbitrary point on a scale, not the complete absence of heat energy. That makes temperature an Interval scale.

Now consider money: An employee earning $\$100,000$ earns exactly twice as much as someone earning $\$50,000$, and $\$0$ represents the complete absence of money. That makes salary a Ratio scale.

05. Population ($N$) vs. Sample ($n$) (Why We Need Samples)

1. Notation and Conceptual Difference

In statistical literature and data science interviews, notation is strictly standardized:

Concept Standard Notation Definition Exit Poll Analogy
Population Capital $N$ The complete collection of all elements, individuals, or items of interest. All 1.5 million registered voters in the state of Goa.
Sample Small $n$ A subset or slice of observations drawn directly from the population. A group of 2,000 randomly surveyed voters leaving voting booths.

2. Why Can't We Just Survey the Entire Population?

  • Physical Impossibility: Election reporters cannot interview all 1.5 million people on voting day—voters are traveling, busy, or unreachable.
  • Prohibitive Cost & Time: A national census takes a full decade and billions of dollars; businesses need answers in days.
  • Destructive Testing: When testing how much impact a car airbag can take before bursting, you cannot crash 100% of the factory's cars! You must test a small sample $n$.

06. The 4 Fundamental Sampling Techniques

How we extract sample $n$ from population $N$ determines whether our models will be accurate or fatally biased. The 4 major sampling methods are:

1. Simple Random Sampling

Every single individual in the population has an equal and independent probability of being selected.

Mechanism: Lottery draw or random number generator np.random.choice().
Use Case: Homogeneous populations, quick exit polling.
2. Stratified Sampling

The population is split into non-overlapping strata (subgroups) based on a key feature, and samples are pulled from each stratum.

Mechanism: Divide by age (0-10, 10-20, 20-40, 40+), gender, or profession; sample proportionally.
Use Case: RBI household income survey; medical clinical trials.
3. Systematic Sampling

From an ordered population, select every $k$-th individual (where $k = N / n$).

Mechanism: Standing outside a store and surveying every 8th person who walks out.
Fun Analogy: The Thanos finger snap selecting every 2nd being!
4. Convenience / Voluntary Sampling

Samples chosen based on ease of access or individuals volunteering because of domain expertise.

Mechanism: Asking only data science professionals for feedback on Python AI libraries.
Caveat: Highly vulnerable to voluntary-response and selection bias!
Real-World Case Study: Clinical Drug Trials

Suppose a pharmaceutical company develops a new cardiac drug. Can they use Simple Random Sampling on the general public?

No! You cannot test a heart medication on healthy 8-year-old children or people without the target condition. Medical researchers use Stratified Sampling: segmenting by age group (>18 years, 40-60, 60+), biological sex, and pre-existing medical histories to guarantee safe and statistically valid outcomes.

07. Frequency Distributions & Cumulative Frequency

1. The Frequency Distribution Table

A Frequency Distribution is a structured table showing the occurrence count of each unique value or category in a dataset. Consider a sample dataset of flowers collected in a botanic survey:

[Rose, Lily, Sunflower, Rose, Lily, Lily, Rose, Lily, Sunflower]

Flower Category Raw Frequency ($f$) Relative Frequency ($f / n$) Cumulative Frequency ($cf$)
Rose 3 $3 / 9 pprox 33.3\%$ 3
Lily 4 $4 / 9 pprox 44.4\%$ $3 + 4 = \mathbf{7}$
Sunflower 2 $2 / 9 pprox 22.2\%$ $7 + 2 = \mathbf{9}$ (Total Sample Size $n$)
What is Cumulative Frequency Used For?

Cumulative Frequency successively adds running counts down the table. In data analysis, cumulative frequency forms the bedrock for calculating Percentiles, Median, and cumulative distribution curves (CDF).

08. Visualizing Data: Bar Charts vs. Histograms

1. The Crucial Distinction

One of the most frequent junior interview mistakes is confusing a Bar Chart with a Histogram:

Feature Bar Chart Histogram
Data Type Categorical / Discrete (e.g. Flower types, Brands, Gender). Continuous / Quantitative (e.g. Age, Salary, Weight, Temperature).
Gaps Between Bars Yes. Gaps emphasize that categories are discrete and separate. No. Bars touch to reflect a continuous numeric spectrum.
X-Axis Representation Distinct labels / names. Continuous numerical intervals called Bins (e.g. 10-20, 20-30).
Y-Axis Representation Count, Frequency, or Percentage. Frequency Count or Probability Density.
Reordering Bars Bars can be rearranged alphabetically or by frequency without breaking meaning. Cannot be rearranged! Order is strictly dictated by numerical magnitude.

2. Histogram Anatomy: Bins and Bin Width

Given age data: [10, 12, 14, 18, 24, 26, 30, 35, 36, 37, 40, 41, 42, 43, 50, 51]. We partition the range into continuous Bins of width 10:

Bin Interval Data Points in Bin Frequency (Bar Height)
[10, 20) 10, 12, 14, 18 4
[20, 30) 24, 26 2
[30, 40) 30, 35, 36, 37 4
[40, 50) 40, 41, 42, 43 4
[50, 60) 50, 51 2

09. Probability Density Functions (PDF) & KDE

1. What is a Probability Density Function (PDF)?

A blocky histogram shows discrete bin counts. What happens if our sample size $n$ grows to infinity while bin width shrinks to zero? The stair-stepped histogram morphs into a smooth continuous mathematical curve: the Probability Density Function (PDF)!

What is Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)?

KDE (Kernel Density Estimation) is a non-parametric technique that places a smooth bell-shaped curve (a Gaussian kernel) over each individual data point and sums them all up to create a smooth, continuous estimate of the underlying PDF.

In Python's Seaborn library, writing sns.histplot(data, kde=True) automatically overlays this smooth KDE curve over your histogram!

2. Fundamental Invariants of a PDF

  • Non-negativity: For all $x$, $f(x) \ge 0$. The curve never drops below the horizontal axis.
  • Total Area Invariant: The total area under the entire PDF curve from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ is strictly equal to $1$ ($100\%$ probability): $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\, dx = 1$$
  • Probability of an Interval: The probability that variable $X$ falls between values $a$ and $b$ equals the integral (area under curve) from $a$ to $b$: $$P(a \le X \le b) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\, dx$$

Concept Core Definition Key Characteristic Standard Example
