Types of Neural Networks, History of Deep Learning & AI Winters

In this lecture, Professor Nitish Singh explains why there is no single "universal" neural network architecture and categorizes the field across data modalities (tabular, spatial grid, sequential, and generative). He computes the mathematical proof of why ANNs fail on computer vision, and walks through the dramatic 70-year history of Deep Learning—from McCulloch-Pitts and Frank Rosenblatt's Perceptron to the crushing AI Winters and the 2012 AlexNet GPU revolution.

Watch Official Lecture on YouTube ↗

01. Modality-Specific Architectures: No Free Lunch

Nitish begins by dispelling a frequent misconception among new students: "Can't we just build one giant neural network and feed it everything?"

The answer is No. Different data structures possess fundamentally different geometric symmetries and properties:

  • Tabular Data (Rows & Columns): Each column has an independent semantic meaning (e.g. Age, Salary, CreditScore). Column order is arbitrary.
  • Spatial Grid Data (Images & Video): Pixels only make sense relative to their spatial neighbors. If you shuffle the pixels of a face, the face is destroyed.
  • Sequential Data (Text, Audio, Time-Series): Data points unfold along a time dimension. Order is everything ("Dog bites man" vs "Man bites dog").

Because of these differences, Deep Learning developed specialized architectural families designed to exploit these inductive biases.

02. The Three Core Foundational Architectures

1. ANN / MLP
Artificial Neural Networks

Feedforward multi-layer perceptrons where every neuron in layer $l$ is fully connected to every neuron in layer $l+1$. Best suited for tabular tabular datasets, structured features, and classical regression/classification.

2. CNN
Convolutional Neural Networks

Uses sliding convolutional filters and pooling layers. Enforces local connectivity, weight sharing, and translation invariance. The undisputed foundation of Computer Vision (object detection, segmentation).

3. RNN / LSTM / GRU
Recurrent Neural Networks

Features recurrent loops where hidden states are passed forward across time steps $h_t = f(h_{t-1}, x_t)$. Possesses internal memory to handle variable-length sequences (Speech, Text, Financial time-series).

03. The Mathematical Proof: Why ANNs Fail on Images

Nitish performs a classic derivation on the board that explains why Yann LeCun had to invent CNNs:

The Parameter Explosion Calculation

Suppose you have a standard modest-resolution color photograph: $$1000 \text{ pixels (Height)} \times 1000 \text{ pixels (Width)} \times 3 \text{ color channels (RGB)}$$ To feed this image into a standard fully connected Artificial Neural Network (ANN), you must first flatten the 2D matrix into a 1D vector: $$\text{Input Vector Size } D = 1000 \times 1000 \times 3 = \mathbf{3,000,000} \text{ input features}$$ Now, suppose your first hidden layer contains just 1,000 neurons (a very modest layer). Because an ANN is fully connected, every input neuron connects to every hidden neuron: $$\text{Number of Weights } W = 3,000,000 \times 1,000 = \mathbf{3,000,000,000} \text{ (3 Billion Parameters!)}$$

The Two Disastrous Consequences:

  1. Catastrophic Overfitting: 3 billion weights on a single layer will immediately memorize the training set, giving 100% training accuracy and near 0% validation accuracy.
  2. Loss of 2D Spatial Locality: Flattening pixels into a 1D vector destroys the relationship between neighboring pixels. Pixel $(0, 0)$ is now separated from pixel $(1, 0)$ by 1,000 positions!

05. Modern Specialized Neural Architectures

Beyond the big three, Nitish introduces the modern architectures that dominate cutting-edge AI:

Autoencoders (Unsupervised)

Encoder compresses high-dimensional input into a tiny bottleneck latent space vector $z$; Decoder reconstructs original input. Used for dimensionality reduction, image denoising, and anomaly detection.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

Invented by Ian Goodfellow (2014). Two networks in a game-theoretic minimax duel: Generator generates fake images, Discriminator acts as an art detective trying to spot fakes.

Transformers (Attention-Based)

Replaced RNNs in modern NLP. Discards recurrent loops entirely in favor of parallel Self-Attention. The core engine behind GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, BERT, and LLaMA.

06. The 70-Year Rollercoaster History of Deep Learning

Deep Learning did not happen overnight. Nitish walks through the dramatic history of euphoria and despair that shaped the field:

1943: The McCulloch-Pitts Neuron

Neurophysiologist Warren McCulloch and logician Walter Pitts propose the first computational model of a biological neuron using binary threshold logic.

1957: Frank Rosenblatt's Perceptron

Rosenblatt builds the physical Mark I Perceptron machine at Cornell. The New York Times boldly reports that the Navy expects perceptrons will soon "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce themselves, and be conscious of their existence."

1969: Minsky & Papert's XOR Proof → THE FIRST AI WINTER

MIT luminaries Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert publish "Perceptrons", mathematically proving that a single-layer perceptron cannot even compute an XOR gate. US government (DARPA) cuts funding; research freezes for over a decade.

1986: The Backpropagation Renaissance

Geoffrey Hinton, David Rumelhart, and Ronald Williams publish their Nature paper showing that multi-layer perceptrons can be trained using the backpropagation chain rule, shattering Minsky's limitation.

Late 1990s: The Second AI Winter

Computers are still too slow to train deep networks. Vanishing gradients cripple deep layers. Corinna Cortes and Vladimir Vapnik's Support Vector Machines (SVM) become mathematically elegant and dominate industry.

2012: The AlexNet Revolution & Modern Boom

Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton enter AlexNet into the ImageNet competition, crushing traditional computer vision by dropping top-5 error rate from 26% to 15.3%. The deep learning gold rush begins.
